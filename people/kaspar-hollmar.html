Kaspar Hollmar was the head of House Hollmar, the Ostvald house that failed around 1458 AS after it accepted a debt repaid in full. Before the bailiffs came for him he stood up at the king's audience and told the court to look at the woman holding its book. Then he vanished, and his family went into bond without him. It is said in Aldrecht, and was seen in 1488 AS, that he is in Veleh, among the brothers of the Rookery, and has not spoken since. He is also said to know the one thing the Ostvald houses most need nobody to know: how a name is struck.

The banker

Before the failure Hollmar was a careful, unremarkable banker who had inherited his house's hall on the Ostvald and its seat among the crown's bankers. He was known for reading every bill himself. When the lord of Varnholt offered to repay his grandfather's loan in full, Hollmar read the bill, weighed the coin and released four hundred tenants, because the bill said he must.

Within a season every Hollmar seal in Bloria had rotted. His family went into the Bond of Aldrecht. Before they did, he went to the Burg and spoke the words the court still murmurs: She held the book for your grandfather. She held it for your father. Look at her. See The Hollmar Failure and The Lady of the Ledger.

The season of rot

Hollmar spent the months of his house's failure watching his bills die. Factors sent them home from every town in Bloria, and he had each one brought to him as it came. Men who worked in the hall that season said he sat up at night with the rotten bills laid out on the counting-tables in rows, reading the names on their backs and comparing them against his own copies of the entries in the crown's book.

By the reckoners' rule a seal holds only while its debtor's name stands in the book. See Blood-Seals. What Hollmar is supposed to have learned from his rows of dead paper is that the rule runs both ways: that a name can be struck from the book from the bill's end, without the book, without the Privy Roll, and without the keeper. How, nobody who repeats the story claims to know. It is the reason the story is repeated at all. A man who can strike a name can release any debtor in Bloria, or rot any house's paper, from anywhere.

Whether he learned anything of the kind, or only became convinced he had, is unknown. The houses have never admitted that the question exists.

The flight

He left Aldrecht on the night of the audience, before the bailiffs arrived. His wife, his three children and some forty others of his household were taken in his place. Nobody is known to have helped him. The likeliest road south was the drovers' road over Crow's Pass, fourteen years before it had a garrison. Nothing more was heard of him in Bloria for about a decade.

From around 1470 AS it was said in the bean-houses of Aldrecht that Hollmar was in Veleh, and that he had gone among the mute brothers. Some Blorians took this as a joke about a man who should have kept his mouth shut. Others took it as plain sense. A man who has said one thing he cannot unsay, and knows another he must never say, could do worse than a house whose rule forbids speech for life. The rule has one penalty: a brother who speaks is taken over the border and left in Mechil.

His family's bills were never cashed. In 1465 AS the Hollmar debtors still in the Bond of Aldrecht were sent north to Kalderhof. Whether he learned of it is not known.

The embassy

In the spring of 1488 AS Baron Mihail met the envoys of Mechil in the Rookery's guest hall. See The Mechil Embassy. Among the brothers who served bread was a very old man with a northern face, who men of the escort say walked like a man who had once been used to being bowed to.

On the second day, according to one man of the escort, the old brother stopped at the table where Mihail's secretary had laid out his papers. Among them were Ostvald bills, drawn on Bloria for Mihail's expenses. The brother turned one over, read the name on the back for some time, and drew his thumbnail once across it. Then he went on serving.

The secretary noticed nothing until the party had left the forest. When he next opened the case, the seal on that bill had gone black and soft, and the paper behind it was spotted brown. The other bills were sound. The bill was destroyed as worthless. Nobody in the escort had read the name on its back.

The identification of the old brother as Kaspar Hollmar rests on the Aldrecht rumour, the man's age and northern looks, and the bill. It is unconfirmed. If it is right, he has not lost what he learned. He has only stopped saying it.

Loose threads

  • Somewhere in Bloria a debtor's bill rotted on a table in the Rookery. Was that debtor freed, or only struck?
  • If the story reaches Aldrecht, what will the houses send into a monastery of men who cannot answer questions?
  • Hollmar has never spoken since. Has he written, and if a mute brother wrote, who would he trust to read it?
