Aldrecht is the crown city of Bloria, a walled residence city at the head of the Olta, north of the range. It is the seat of the king and his household, of the Ostvald banking houses and of the largest debtors' prison in the empire. It is famous for its ceremony, its music, its bread and its quiet. Two Ustrus armies have died under its walls. Its people say the city has never been taken, and that it has never needed to be: everything in it is already owed.

The city

Aldrecht lies in a broad green basin where a dozen springs run together to become the Olta. The river leaves the basin southward through a gorge in the range, passes under Crow's Pass, and comes out in Veleh as the water that feeds the swamps of Tullanon. Boatmen in Tullanon say the Olta brings two things down from Bloria: snowmelt and paper.

The old city stands inside a ring of grey stone walls. Beyond them lies a wide field kept bare of houses, trees and hedges, so that the guns on the walls have nothing to hide behind. Beyond the field is a second ring, low, thick and starred with bastions, built after the second siege. The suburbs outside it are newer, poorer and much larger. In a city of this size the streets are oddly calm. Travellers from Veleh remark on the swept cobbles, the street-lamps, the bells, and the number of shuttered windows in otherwise good houses.

The court has given Aldrecht a taste for ceremony that runs down to the shopkeepers. Titles are used in full. Precedence is kept at table, in church and in the queue for bread. Southern travellers find this absurd. Blorians find it orderly, and point out that a city in which everybody knows exactly where he stands is a city in which nobody needs to be told.

The Burg

The royal residence, called simply the Burg, fills the south-west corner of the old city. It was never planned. Each reign has added a wing, a court, a chapel or a stair, and no reign has torn anything down, so the Burg is a town of its own: some twenty courtyards, eighteen chapels by one count, and corridors that lead into rooms nobody living has a key to. Ambassadors are walked through it by a longer road than they need, which is understood to be the point.

Court etiquette in the Burg is rigid and old. Nobody touches the king. Nobody speaks to him before he speaks. Nobody leaves a room with their back to him. Every person at court holds a rank in the household, and every rank has a door it may use and doors it may not.

One custom of the Burg is better known outside it than in. When a reign's senior household officer retires, the rooms she lived in are not given to anyone else. Her furniture is left in place, the door is locked, and the key is returned to the Privy Roll. Servants say there is a sealed suite of this kind in every wing of the Burg, one for each reign, and that they are dusted. Who dusts them is unknown. See The Lady of the Ledger.

The Ostvald and the Bonds

The Ostvald is the exchange street of Aldrecht: a steep, narrow lane of tall stone counting-halls running down from the cathedral square toward the river gate. It gives its name to the Ostvald houses, the five banking families who keep halls there. Two further halls stand shuttered. They belonged to the houses that failed within living memory (see The Hollmar Failure), and their doors have been sealed with the same red wax the houses use on their bills.

Below the Ostvald, against the river wall, stands the great Bond of Aldrecht. It is the largest debtors' prison in Bloria and the first stage of the north road. Every month, on a night the city does not announce, closed wagons leave its yard by the north gate for Kalderhof. The streets they use are kept clear by custom, not by order. Aldrecht people draw their shutters at the sound of wheels after dark and do not look out.

Every Ostvald bill that leaves Aldrecht comes back to it in the end. The bills that fund Baron Mihail are drawn on Ostvald halls, countersigned in Ocristan by Edrun Vasselt, and cashed in Aldrecht. When the houses wanted to know why some of them came back by strange hands, they sent a reckoner south from here. See Wendel Raust.

The sieges

Ustrus has laid siege to Aldrecht twice. The first siege, around 1230 AS, ended when the Ustrus camp broke up in autumn with the walls unbreached; the reason given in Blorian annals is the weather. The second, in 1384 AS, lasted two months. The Ustrus army undermined the inner wall in three places and was within a day of taking the city when a relief army came over the northern hills and broke the camp.

Who raised and paid that relief army is a point of Blorian pride and of quiet Blorian doubt. The annals say it was raised by the crown. The Ostvald houses hold the bills. The crown is still paying the interest on them, a century after the last of the soldiers died.

The Ustrus camp left behind sacks of a roasted southern bean. Aldrecht learned to brew it, and the bean-houses where it is drunk are now where the city does its talking. Factors, reckoners, clerks and spies sit in them from morning to night. A great deal of what Bloria knows about Veleh is first heard there.

The crypt door

The kings of Bloria are buried in three places. Their hearts go in silver urns to the household chapel in the Burg. Their entrails go to the cathedral vaults. Their bodies go to the crypt of a small mendicant church near the Ostvald, which has no other distinction.

The burial follows a fixed form. The funeral procession stops at the crypt door, and the court herald knocks. A voice from inside asks who seeks entry. The herald answers with the dead king's name and every one of his titles, and the door stays shut. He knocks again and answers with the king's victories, and the door stays shut. He knocks a third time and answers: a debtor of the crown. Then the door is opened.

Blorian preachers read the rite as a lesson in humility. Others note that the voice inside the door is not a priest's and that its owner has never been named in the rolls.

The court in 1488 AS

The king of Bloria in 1488 AS is Eberwin III, crowned in 1461 AS. He is past seventy and in good health. He is held to be the cleverest king Bloria has had in a century and one of the hardest to read. He has fought few wars and lost none of them. He has married his children and grandchildren into every court that would take them, so that half the thrones in the north have a Blorian at the table. A Blorian saying runs: Ustrus conquers; Bloria marries, and lends.

Eberwin signs very little in his own name. The payments to Mihail go through the Ostvald houses and their factor, not the crown, so that if Mihail fails, the houses lose the money and the crown loses nothing it can be blamed for. Mihail killed a Blorian marcher lord's riders at Crow's Pass in 1472 AS. Eberwin began paying him some years later, and has never been heard to mention the battle.

Lady Hedwen, Mistress of the Privy Roll, held the book at Eberwin's coronation twenty-seven years ago. Courtiers say she has not changed since. A senior household officer retires late in each reign, so Aldrecht watches Hedwen the way other capitals watch the king's colour. Twice in the last decade it has been said in the bean-houses that the king was dying. Both times Hedwen did nothing, and both times the king recovered within the week. Some at court think Eberwin started both rumours himself, to see what she would do. Whether he learned anything is unknown.

Loose threads

  • A sealed suite in the Burg for every reign. Does the number of locked doors match the number of coronations in the rolls, or is there one more?
  • The voice behind the crypt door admits a dead king only as a debtor. To whom does a king of Bloria die owing?
  • Eberwin twice let it be said he was dying, and watched. What was he looking for, and what would he do if he found it?
  • The relief army of 1384 AS was paid on Ostvald bills. Whose names were on the backs of them, and where are those debtors now?
