Vadra

Realm
Veleh, in Strova, on the Eastern Sea south of the Vorn Danu mouth
Ruled by
The Benches: a compact of boat-owners. No lord has held the town in living memory.
Lives by
Herring, salt, and carrying what others will not carry openly
Known for
The landing of the Ustrus gold, 1487 AS. See The Gold Run.
Holds
Eleven brass tallies taken from drowned men, by report

Vadra is a fishing town on the Strova coast, a little south of the Vorn Danu mouth. It has no lord and no wall. It is run by the Benches, a compact of the men who own its boats, who sell the harbour to whoever pays. In 1487 AS they were paid to land the Ustrus gold. In the weeks after, the sea brought the galley dead ashore below the town, and some of them wore brass tallies of the Counting-House sewn into their collars.

The town

Vadra stands on a shingle bank between a salt marsh and the sea, with its back to the delta of the Vorn Danu. It is a town of tarred sheds, drying racks and boats drawn up above the tide line. Its one stone building is the salt-house, where the Benches meet. The road inland to Ocristan is a cart track across the marsh, impassable for part of each spring.

Ustrus has never kept an officer here. The town pays its tax in salt fish, through a broker in Ocristan, and the riders seldom come so far.

The Benches

The Benches are the boat-owners of Vadra, about a dozen families, who sit on the benches of the salt-house to settle the town's business. They own the boats, the drying racks and the harbour. They are not elected and do not pretend to be. Any family that buys a boat may sit, and none has been refused in living memory; families that lose their boats leave the benches.

The Benches carry for anyone. They have run wool to Mechil, wine from Ustrus, and men who did not wish to be seen on the roads. They do not ask what a cargo is, and they do not sell a client's name to the next client. That is the whole of their reputation, and they guard it.

The landing

In 1487 AS two broad coasters came in from the south with Baron Mihail and the Hundred-Eleven aboard and the Ustrus gold under a false floor. The Benches had been paid to receive them. The payment came weeks in advance, in Ustrus silver, through a middleman the Benches will not name. The gold went inland on a separate wagon and reached Ocristan. See The Gold Run.

The tallies

On the voyage north the coasters had been attacked by two flagless galleys of southern build, which were burned. Their dead went over the side unsearched. Over the following weeks the current brought bodies ashore along the shingle below Vadra, singly and in twos, perhaps thirty in all.

Most were oarsmen, still in the chains they had been cut free of or had died in. A number of them, eleven by the most repeated account, had a small brass tally sewn into the collar of the shirt. The tallies were stamped with a mark of the Counting-House and a number. Tallies of that kind are given to people taken on the house's second ledger, the levy of people, so that they can be found in its books. The galleys had been rowed by men the Counting-House had taken.

At least one of the eleven was a Vadra man. He had been taken for the oars eight years before, when his family could not meet the fish tax, and his mother knew his face.

The Benches buried the dead in the marsh without a priest and told nobody. The tallies were cut from the collars and kept. Who keeps them is unknown.

What the tallies show

If the tallies are what they appear to be, the galleys that attacked the gold belonged to, or were crewed from, the Counting-House. That supports the reading that the house acted against the Emperor's own loan. See The Counting-House.

It is also possible that someone sent galleys rowed by Counting-House levies precisely so that the house would be blamed if any were found. Galleys change hands, and so do oarsmen. This is disputed.

The man asking

In 1488 AS a stranger came to Vadra by the marsh road. He spoke the northern tongue with a southern accent and asked the Benches, politely and at length, whether any drowned men had come ashore the year before. The Benches told him the sea off Vadra keeps what it takes. He paid for his lodging in good silver and left inland. Whose man he was is unknown.

Loose threads

  • The Benches took Ustrus silver for the landing through a middleman. Who arranged it, weeks before the Emperor had agreed to pay?
  • Who holds the eleven tallies, and what are they worth, and to whom?
  • The stranger who came asking: was he sent to find the evidence, or to bury it?
