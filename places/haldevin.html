Haldevin was a village in a mill valley in Veleh's western foothills. It was one of the villages the Hundred-Eleven turned against the tax riders in 1487–1488 AS, and it sent its men to the rising at Trantar. Few of them came home. When the riders came back, they burned it.

The valley

Haldevin was small and off the road. It had a mill on the stream, a low stone church, an inn, and barns that smelled of last year's hay. Four families decided what the village did. The eldest of their heads, Borek, spoke for them. Most maps of Veleh did not mark it. The tax ledgers did.

Under the press

Twice a year three riders came in town livery with two leather boxes and a ledger. They took half of everything the households could spare, and a little more when the ledger said so. Nobody fought them. A man called Janos refused to weigh his grain in front of them and was beaten in the square with fists and a stick. He walked home with help and died in the night.

The children coughed every winter. Some years the coughing stopped and some years it stopped because the child had. In the villages under Olegzander's press, people said the children sickened when the riders came hardest, and some blamed his witch. In Haldevin, at least, no witch was needed. Half the grain taken twice a year is enough to kill children in a cold valley.

The squad, 1487–1488 AS

In late 1487 AS Dean Farrick brought twenty-six men of the Hundred-Eleven down into the valley before dark. They said they were deserters from the eastern wars, paying their way, and they kept their branded hands gloved. They were billeted in the barns and helped with the harvest, and they drilled in the hayloft where the village boys could watch. The watching was the point.

Farrick put the question to the four heads of household at the inn: what if a man did not have to give half? Borek answered that the real question was whether a man kept his roof. Then the riders came again, and the squad sat on the church wall and did nothing, and the village saw that too.

In the spring the riders came six strong, and one of them hit a girl in the square. Borek stepped forward and was hit as well. That rider came back alone some days later for something he had forgotten. Borek's son, fifteen and with a stutter, met him at the gate and killed him. After that the village had no way back. The four families and eight men from the next valley joined the rising. Borek made the speech, because Farrick would not.

Trantar and after

Haldevin's men marched south and west with the squad. They joined other villages at a fork and reached Trantar about four hundred strong. On the third day of the battle the peasant wings did not form up. Borek was somewhere in the field, and nobody from the company saw him again. The company got out through the eastern gate. The villagers mostly did not.

The riders came back to the valley that year, this time with soldiers. They burned the mill, the barns and the inn. The stone church still stands, roofless. Before the burning they took the church register: births, marriages and burials going back generations, every name in the valley. Why they wanted it is not known. Most of the women and children who were left went to kin in the next valleys. Some did not get that far.

Loose threads

  • The riders took the register: every name in the valley, living and dead. Who holds it now, and what is a list of names for? Answered in part: the Counting-House, to find the survivors and take them as a levy of people.
  • Borek's son killed a tax rider at fifteen. He is not among the known dead.
  • Three other Deans had valleys of their own: two villages, four young valleys, a river country. Were they all burned?
