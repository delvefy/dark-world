The breaking of the bridge is the event that left the builders' great span over the Vorn Danu as a row of legs in the current. Before it, a single bridge of white kimberstone crossed the river from bank to bank. A beaten army retreating north broke it behind itself. Its mages paid for the breaking with a town: the people of Ostrel, at the northern end of the bridge, every one. The war, the army and its pursuers are all forgotten. The town is remembered only because the river goes still for it, one night every year.

The bridge

The builders' bridge was a single span of kimberstone about a kilometre long, raised on legs that stand in the current to this day. It was built before the Silence, like all the builders' work, and like all of it had no joints. The road came down to it on the northern bank through the town of Ostrel, which lived on the crossing. At the southern end the road passed under a freestanding kimberstone arch before it left the span.

The breaking

What is known comes from three short entries in old annals, which agree on little except the outcome. The account they share runs as follows.

An army that had gone south across the river came back north across it, beaten, with a pursuit close behind. Whose army it was, and whose the pursuit, the entries do not agree. The army crossed. Its mages then broke the bridge behind it, while the pursuers were still on the southern bank.

Kimberstone yields to no tool and no ordinary magic. The one other break on record, the seventh tower of the Rift, cost the mage Aran his own soul. A span a kilometre long cost more. The army's mages did not pay with their own lives. They paid with Ostrel's.

The army's soldiers drove the people of Ostrel out of their houses and onto the bridge, to the middle of the span. The oldest entry says only the town was spent. The second gives a figure, which is illegible. The third says the people were made to kneel facing south, and that the mages walked among them. When they were done the span fell in a single piece, with the people of Ostrel on it, into the river. The army marched north. The town was never rebuilt.

How many died is unknown. Estimates from the size of Ostrel's foundations, which can still be traced in the fields above the crossing, run from several hundred to some thousands.

The southern arch

On the Ustrus bank, where the span once came down, the arch still stands. It is the same kind of work as the Crow Gate at Crow's Pass: a single arch of white kimberstone, about four times the height of a man, with no wall on either side of it and nothing attached. The road no longer passes under it. It stands inside the walls of a small Ustrus post on the bank, which the modern road bends around.

The post is garrisoned, and nobody who is not of its garrison is allowed through its gate. Travellers on the Ustrus road see the top of the arch over the wall. In 1487 AS Baron Mihail's column passed it on the way to Beit Haifud. See The Gold Run. What the post is for, beyond keeping people away from the arch, is unknown. Whether anyone has ever been laid across its sill, as Mihail was across the sill at Crow's Pass, is unknown.

The still night

One night every year, in the late autumn, the Vorn Danu at the crossing goes still. Between the old legs, from bank to bank, the water stops moving on its surface. It does not freeze. It goes flat and makes no sound, while the river above and below the crossing runs and foams as usual. By dawn it is moving again.

The ferrymen and toll-keepers at the crossing hold that it is the night of the breaking, and that the river is keeping still for Ostrel. On that night the toll-keepers close the timber deck, and the ferries are drawn up on both banks. Nobody goes on the water. A boatman who once did, for a wager, said afterward that he heard nothing, not even his own oars, and that the water held the boat up like a floor. He did not take the wager a second time.

What the stillness is, and whether it is anything more than a river's habit, is unknown. In 1487 AS, in a fight at sea during the Gold Run, the water around Baron Mihail went flat in a ring while the sea beyond it kept rolling. Men of the escort who had crossed the Vorn Danu with him have compared the two. Nobody has explained either.
