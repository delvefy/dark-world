Event · c. 1458 AS

The Hollmar Failure

Date
c. 1458 AS, in the last years of the reign before Eberwin III's
Place
The Ostvald, Aldrecht
House
Hollmar, then one of seven Ostvald houses
Cause
It accepted a debt repaid in full and released the debtors
Outcome
Its bills failed; its debts were bought by the other houses; the family went into bond
Head of the house
Kaspar Hollmar. Fled; said to be a mute brother in Veleh.

The Hollmar failure is the collapse of one of the seven Ostvald banking houses around 1458 AS. House Hollmar did the one thing the Ostvald houses are known never to do: it accepted the full repayment of a loan and let the people pledged against it go free. Within a year its bills had failed, its debts had been bought by the other houses, and every member of the family who could be found was in bond. Its head, Kaspar Hollmar, said something aloud at court before he fled that Bloria has not forgotten.

The repayment

About 1458 AS the lord of Varnholt, a small marcher lordship in the hills west of Aldrecht, came into money. The source is variously given as a silver find, a dowry and a sale of timber rights. He owed House Hollmar a loan his grandfather had taken, and against its interest about four hundred of his tenants were in the Bonds.

The lord offered to repay the whole of it, principal and interest, in coin. Ostvald practice was, and is, to refuse. The houses find a reason: a fault in the coin, a clause in the bill, a sum of interest that has run on since the offer was made. Hollmar did not refuse. Kaspar Hollmar read the bill, had the coin weighed, and found that it was paid. The law of the bill says that when the debt is paid the debtor is released. He released them.

Why he did so is disputed. His defenders said he was an honest banker who kept to the letter of his paper. His enemies said he meant to buy a lord's gratitude and a seat on the crown council. Nobody suggested he had done it out of pity, and he never claimed to.

The bills

Every Ostvald bill is sealed in a debtor's blood and can only be cashed by the person it names. A forged seal is said to rot. Within a season of the release, Hollmar seals began to fail. Genuine bills, drawn on the house and held by their rightful payees, were found with the red wax gone black and soft and the paper behind it spotted brown, as though they were forgeries. Merchants stopped taking Hollmar paper. Factors abroad sent their Hollmar bills home and asked for coin. The house could not pay them all.

The other six houses offered to buy Hollmar's debts at a price set by themselves. The crown approved the sale. Under the law of the Ostvald a house that cannot meet its bills is a debtor like any other, and its people are its collateral. Kaspar Hollmar's wife, his three children, his brothers and their households, his clerks and his servants went into the Bond of Aldrecht. About forty people are named in the record.

The tenants of Varnholt were free for a little under two years. The lord who had paid could get no credit anywhere in Bloria afterward, and no merchant would buy his timber. He borrowed again, from House Brede, on its terms. By 1461 AS his villages were back in bond.

The audience

Before the bailiffs came for him, Kaspar Hollmar went to the Burg. He held a household office as one of the crown's bankers and had the right to attend the king's public audience. He waited his turn, and when it came he did not petition. He turned toward the household officers who stood behind the throne. The record of the audience gives his words only in summary. The court's own memory gives them in full, and nobody repeats them above a murmur: She held the book for your grandfather. She held it for your father. Look at her.

The audience ended. No one answered him. The officer he spoke of was the keeper of the seals of that reign, a woman of middle years with pale eyes and a low voice. She is recorded as having said nothing at all. She retired to the country late the following year, as such officers do, and early in the next reign Lady Hedwen held the book at Eberwin III's coronation. See The Lady of the Ledger.

Kaspar Hollmar was not in his house when the bailiffs came that night. His family was. He has not been seen in Bloria since. He is one of the two courtiers known to have spoken of the Lady aloud within the last century, and the only one who did not go into bond himself.

Afterward

The Hollmar hall on the Ostvald was shut and sealed with the red wax the houses use on their bills. It still stands so, one of two shuttered halls on the street. The family's names were entered in the Bond rolls in the ordinary way. None of them was ransomed. Seven years after the failure, in 1465 AS, the Hollmar debtors whose bills had not been cashed were moved to the lower bond and taken north to Kalderhof. Kaspar Hollmar's youngest child would have been about fourteen.

Bloria took the lesson the houses meant it to take. A house that accepts repayment fails. A borrower who repays cannot borrow again. In the thirty years since, no Ostvald house is known to have accepted full repayment from anyone. Lords who could pay have stopped trying.

Why the seals failed is unknown. Some say the other houses arranged it, by means nobody can name. Others say the seals were never the houses' to keep, and that whatever makes a blood-seal hold had withdrawn from a house that let its blood go.

Loose threads
