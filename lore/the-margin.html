The Margin

What
Orders that appear, unsigned, in the margins of the Counting-House's second ledger
Where
The ledger hall of the Counting-House, Beit Haifud
Written in
An old form of the southern script that no living clerk writes; brown ink
Obeyed by
Every officer of the house, without question
Head of the house
None. The chair at the top of its rolls is empty.
Source
Chiefly one runaway clerk, 1488 AS. Disputed.

The Margin is what the clerks of the Counting-House call the hand that gives them orders. The house has no head. It has keepers for its ledgers, masters for its riders and stewards for its galleys, and above them an empty chair. Its orders appear in the margins of the second ledger, the ledger of people, written overnight in a hand that has not changed in living memory and belongs to nobody in the building. The house obeys them. Most of what Veleh knows of this comes from one man who ran, and much of it is disputed.

The empty chair

The Counting-House keeps rolls of its officers going back further than the present dynasty. Every office in them is filled in every year except the first. That line carries a title, the Reckoner of the Two Books, and no name. Officers of the house are said to bow to the chair in the ledger hall when they enter and leave, as courtiers bow to a throne.

Veleh assumed for fifty years that the house had a master who preferred not to be seen. The account of the runaway clerk is that it has none, and never has. The keepers of the two ledgers are the highest officers who exist. They do not decide what the house does. They read it.

The hand

The second ledger is kept in a long vaulted hall in the Counting-House at Beit Haifud. Clerks enter names in it by day: villages, households, the levied and the owed. At sunset the hall is shut and the ledger is left open on its stand. Nobody stays in the hall at night.

In the morning there is sometimes writing in the margin that was not there at sunset. It is never much. A name circled. A village struck through. A number beside a household. A single word against a page: now, or wait, or all. The script is an old form of the southern hand, the kind found on tomb-stones, and the ink dries brown. Clerks who have served forty years say the hand has not changed in that time. Ledgers in the house's archive show it, unchanged, in the margins of books two centuries old.

The keepers read the margin each morning and issue orders from it. The riders, the levy-masters and the galley stewards act on those orders without knowing where they came from. Most of the house's officers have never seen the ledger.

What it has ordered

Some of the house's known acts in Veleh can be matched to the clerk's account. None of these is confirmed.

  • Haldevin. The village was circled, by the clerk's account, before the Trantar rising had ended. The riders were ordered to bring back the register before they burned the church.
  • The Rift. During the five weeks of the siege the margin beside Lord Andrej's page was blank. The keepers sent no relief because nothing told them to. The clerk took the silence for a decision.
  • The Gold Run. Baron Mihail's name was circled twice in 1487 AS: once before he crossed the Vorn Danu, and once on the day after his audience with the Emperor. The bloodhounds followed the first mark and the galleys the second.

Who arranged the coasters that carried the gold north, and the false floor built into them weeks before the audience, does not appear in the clerk's account at all.

Those who watched

The house has a rule that nobody stays in the ledger hall after sunset. It is not written down. The clerk says it did not need to be. Within his own service two clerks hid in the hall to see the hand at work. Neither was seen again. In each case, on the next morning, the clerk's own name had been entered on the second ledger in the ordinary way, in an ordinary clerk's hand, as a levy to the oars. Nobody in the hall admitted to writing it.

The clerks of the house are themselves taken on the second ledger, as children with a good hand, and raised inside it. They are not released. That is what made the runaway notable.

The runaway

In 1488 AS a clerk of the second ledger left Beit Haifud, crossed northern Ustrus and was carried up the coast by the boatmen of Vadra, who do not sell a client's name. He came ashore in Strova and sold his account inland. His name is withheld by those who bought it. Whether he is still alive is unknown.

His account has never been tested. He could not have known some of what he described, and he had every reason to make it worth buying. Against that, it explains the house's conduct better than anything else on offer. In particular it explains why the house acted against its own Emperor's loan without ever seeming to argue with him: it was not arguing. It was obeying something else.

His last report from Beit Haifud is the most often repeated. In the spring of 1488 AS, he said, the name of the Emperor Tamarun appeared in the margin. It was not circled and not struck. It was simply written there, alone, with nothing beside it. No clerk in the hall had seen the hand write a name without a mark, and none of them knew what it meant. He left within the month.

Theories

  • The patron's own hand. The one of the Thirteen held to stand behind Ustrus writes the margin, by night and in person. This is the common reading in Veleh, and it fits the house's long refusal to answer to the throne.
  • A bound book. The ledger itself writes. If the second ledger is a relic with its own will, like the Grimoire of the Hundred-Eleven (see Bound Relics), then the house serves a book, and whoever stands behind Ustrus may be served by it in turn, or may not.
  • A hidden officer. There is a Reckoner after all, kept out of the rolls, and the hall is not as empty at night as the clerks believe. The two vanished watchers argue against anyone finding out.

None of these is confirmed. The clerk himself said only that the hand was patient, and that it never corrected itself.

Loose threads

  • The Emperor's name was written in the margin with no mark beside it. What does an unmarked name mean, and has a mark been added since?
  • Where is the runaway clerk, and who bought his account in Strova?
  • If the hand is two centuries old in the archive, how far back do the margins go, and is there a first entry?
