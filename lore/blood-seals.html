A blood-seal is the red wax seal on a bill of the Ostvald houses. The wax holds the blood of one bonded debtor, whose name is written on the back. The seal makes the bill good: only its named payee can cash it, and a forged one rots. What makes a seal hold is not the wax or the blood. Ostvald reckoners are taught, and do not write down, that a seal holds only while its debtor's name stands in the crown's book of debts, the book the king of Bloria signs at his coronation. A debtor whose blood is in a bill can feel, all his life, which way that bill lies.

The sealing

Every large Bond in Bloria has a sealing room. When a house issues a bill, a sealer of the house draws blood from a debtor held against that house's loans, mixes it into warm red wax and seals the bill in front of a witness. The debtor's name is written on the back. One debtor goes to one seal. Large bills are given to debtors expected to live long.

The same day a copy of the name goes up to Aldrecht, to the clerks of the Privy Roll in the Burg. They enter it in the crown's book against the bill's number and the house that drew it. The crown owes the houses more than it can pay, and the bonded are the houses' collateral for that debt, so every bonded name in Bloria is in the book. The office that keeps the book is the household office held in each reign by the woman Blorians call the Lady of the Ledger. In 1488 AS it is the office of Lady Hedwen, Mistress of the Privy Roll.

None of this is secret in itself. The Privy Roll's registry of bonded names is a known crown office. What reckoners are taught is the rest.

What makes it hold

Reckoners of the houses learn three rules in their first year, by word of mouth:

  • A seal holds while the name stands. A bill whose debtor is entered in the book will pass from hand to hand, blister any hand but its payee's, and be honoured at any Ostvald hall.
  • A struck name rots its seal. If the name is struck through in the book, the seal goes black and soft within days, and the paper behind it spots brown. A forged seal rots for the same reason: its name was never entered.
  • A dead name goes to powder. When the debtor dies, the seal dries grey and crumbles in the hand, and the bill is void. The houses owe nothing on it.

This is how Wendel Raust and his kind can audit a bill by looking at it. It is also why reckoners are not supposed to leave the houses' service.

The third rule has a consequence that Blorians who hold large bills prefer not to discuss. A debtor who goes north to Kalderhof and dies there voids his bill. A payee who draws slowly is gambling that the debtor will outlive the delay. The houses are not known to warn anyone of this.

Hollmar

The rules explain the Hollmar failure of c. 1458 AS better than anything else does. When House Hollmar released the four hundred tenants of Varnholt, it asked the Privy Roll to strike their names, as the law of the bill requires. Within a season every Hollmar seal in Bloria had rotted, the genuine with the forged.

The account given by reckoners, and never confirmed, is that the clerks of the Privy Roll struck more than four hundred names. When the book was next opened, the whole of House Hollmar's account had been struck through: every debtor it held and every bill it had drawn, in a single line, in a hand none of the clerks admitted to. The keeper of the book in that reign was the keeper of the seals, the officer Kaspar Hollmar named aloud at court before he fled.

If the account is true, no house fails by bad luck. A house fails when its page is struck.

The pull

A bonded debtor whose blood is in a bill feels where the bill is. It is described as a pull, faint and constant, like a hook under the breastbone. Debtors in the Bonds turn toward their bills in their sleep. Guards say that a long-held prisoner can point to his bill through stone walls, and that whole cells of them lie at night with their heads the same way.

When the bill is cashed the pull stops. The debtor is then supposed to be released. When the bill moves, the pull moves with it. Debtors have been known to weep when they feel their bill carried away south.

In the Bond of Aldrecht, by guards' report, there is a long cell of debtors whose bills fund Baron Mihail. They sleep facing south-east, toward Ocristan. Some of the names on those bills are from Veleh. See Edrun Vasselt.

Two books

Bloria keeps its subjects in a book that a household officer holds and a king signs. Ustrus keeps its subjects in a ledger whose orders appear in the margin overnight. See The Margin. Whether this likeness means anything, or is only what any empire would do with a great many people and a great deal of paper, is unknown.

Loose threads

  • A debtor can feel which way his bill lies. Has any debtor ever followed the pull out of a Bond, and what would he find at the end of it?
  • Who struck the Hollmar page, and can the same hand strike a single bill, or a single factor's?
  • If a bill goes to powder when its debtor dies, how many of the bills Mihail is drawing slowly are already worthless?
